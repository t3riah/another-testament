Another Testament

The Bible on most shelves is one version of a longer library. Here is the whole shelf: how the lists were drawn, why Ethiopia’s is the largest, and what the “lost” books really are.

New to all this? Good. You’re not expected to know these words, and plenty of them look strange or confusing on sight. Everything here is explained in plain language, and the word list right after this screen is there to help.

Canon means a measuring rod

Start with the word itself, which is probably new to you. The Greek word kanon (say “CAN-un”) was a reed used to measure. Applied to scripture, it is the list of books a community measures its faith by. Nobody handed down that list finished. It formed by use, argument and, much later, formal decisions.

No single vote

The Council of Nicaea in 325 argued about Arius and the date of Easter, not about which books to include. Lists came from many places, over centuries.

Used, then listed

Churches read many books long before any list. A book could be loved, quoted and still not listed, and the reverse. Reading, trusting and canonising are three different acts.

Same books, different lines

Most of the disagreement is not about whether these books exist. It is about where the line falls between scripture, useful reading, and church law.

  1. Before AD 100

    A wider library is already in useGreek-speaking Jews read the Septuagint, which includes books later left out of the Hebrew canon. Enoch and Jubilees circulate. Jude quotes Enoch as prophecy.

  2. AD 367

    Athanasius lists the 27His Easter letter gives the first known list that matches today’s New Testament. Regional councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) agree, and include the wider Old Testament books.

  3. About 405

    Jerome’s VulgateJerome prefers the Hebrew list but still translates the extra books, labelling them apart. They stay in Western Bibles for a thousand years.

  4. 1534 to 1646

    The Reformation redraws the Old TestamentLuther moves them to a separate section. The Westminster Confession (1646) says they are not inspired. Rome answers at Trent in 1546 by defining its canon.

  5. 1611

    The King James Bible prints them anywayThe first KJV puts the Apocrypha between the Testaments. They were dropped from Protestant printings later, which is why the 66-book Bible is a shortened edition of the 1611 one.

  6. 1945 and 1947

    Buried libraries returnNag Hammadi in Egypt yields Coptic gospels and more. The Dead Sea Scrolls yield Hebrew and Aramaic copies of Enoch, Jubilees and the whole Second Temple shelf.

Why Ethiopia’s Bible is the biggest

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church (“Tewahedo” means “made one”) never cut its shelf. That one decision is why books lost elsewhere survive whole there.

Christianity reached the kingdom of Aksum (in what is now northern Ethiopia) in the fourth century, and the scriptures were rendered into Ge’ez, an ancient Ethiopian language the church still prays in, in the centuries after. The Greek Old Testament was the base, so the wider books came with it. Enoch and Jubilees were never removed.

The church does not have a single council-fixed itemised list the way Western churches do. The count is usually given as 81: 46 Old Testament plus 35 New Testament, which includes church-order writings (the Sinodos, the Books of the Covenant, the Ethiopic Clement, the Didascalia). A narrower reckoning gets to 81 as 54 plus 27. Counting combined works separately gives 88. That is not a rival canon, only a different way of counting the same shelf.

Outside Ethiopia the Bible’s outer edge was trimmed, so the Ethiopian manuscripts became the only complete witnesses to some books. In 1773 the Scottish traveller James Bruce brought Ge’ez copies of Enoch to Europe, and Richard Laurence’s English translation followed in 1821. Later, Aramaic fragments of Enoch turned up at Qumran and confirmed that the book is ancient.

Four things people get wrong

Short answers to the claims that come up most.

“The Council of Nicaea chose the books of the Bible.”

It did not. Nicaea in 325 dealt with the Arian controversy and the date of Easter. The first known list of the 27 New Testament books is Athanasius’s letter of 367, and the Old Testament line was still being drawn in different places well after that.

“Catholics added extra books at Trent.”

Trent defined what was already in use. Those books were in the Greek Old Testament and the Vulgate for over a thousand years. Luther set them apart in the 1500s, and Trent answered in 1546. Both moves were formal; the underlying books were not new.

“Enoch was cut out of the Bible by the church.”

Mostly it was never in. Neither the Hebrew canon nor most churches listed it, though Jude quotes it and many early writers knew it. Ethiopia kept it as scripture, and that is why the complete text survives.

“The lost gospels are older and truer than Matthew, Mark, Luke and John.”

Mostly later, and disputed. Most of the Nag Hammadi writings are second to fourth century. The Gospel of Thomas is the exception scholars argue about, with dates from the late first to mid second century. Lumping them together as one hidden gospel hides how different they are.

Another Testament is a study companion. Summaries are paraphrased, and dates for several books are debated by scholars. Canon counts for the Ethiopian church follow the traditional 81 and the 88 itemised count; Orthodox lists differ between churches.
